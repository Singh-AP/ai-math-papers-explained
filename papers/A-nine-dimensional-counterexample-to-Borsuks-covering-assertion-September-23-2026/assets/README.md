### Notebook sources

The NotebookLM notebook was built on the second NotebookLM account and has three sources:

1. `paper.pdf`: *A nine-dimensional counterexample to Borsuk's covering assertion* (openai/math, 23 Sep 2026)
2. [`lean/docs/156.md`](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/docs/156.md): scope of the Lean formalization
3. [Wikipedia: Borsuk's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsuk%27s_conjecture), for historical background

The slides, both infographics and the audio used all three sources. The report and the mind map used only sources 1 and 2.

## Errata

NotebookLM is good at structure and visuals but sometimes invents or garbles mathematical detail. Known issues, found by reading each output against the paper:

**`slides.pdf` / `slides.pptx`**

- **Revision.** The deck shipped here is the revised one. One `nlm slides revise` run targeted slides 1, 2, 7, 8 and 15, and `scripts/diff_slides.py` confirmed that no other slide changed. The original deck's problems on those five slides, and what the revision did, are listed below.
- **Slide 1:** *fixed.* The original said "researchers finally found the exact threshold where our geometric intuition breaks down" (the paper says its theorem "does not determine the smallest dimension in which it fails"), and its title "Shattering a 93-Year-Old Geometric Mystery" and "struck down … Borsuk's Conjecture" gave the preprint the credit for the 1993 disproof. The revised slide is titled "Borsuk fails in dimension nine" and says that Kahn and Kalai disproved the conjecture in high dimensions in 1993, that the preprint lowered the smallest known failing dimension from 63 to 9, and that the smallest failing dimension lies between 4 and 9. No errors found in the revised slide.
- **Slide 2:** *improved, still imperfect.* The original's magenta "Diameter = √3 r" segment ran from the centre of the disk to the rim, a length of $r$. In the revised slide it is a chord of the slice, but it ends at about 3 o'clock instead of at the far end of the 120° arc (about 4 o'clock), so it is shorter than the true diameter $\sqrt3\ r$; the line also runs through the word "Diameter". The text is right.
- **Slide 3:** no errors found.
- **Slide 4:** no errors found. The shapes are schematic.
- **Slide 5:** no errors found. The background picture is decorative.
- **Slide 6:** the chart is garbled in places. The first point is labelled "1325 & 946 (Nilli)" and the 946 point has no label; these are two separate records (Kahn–Kalai 1993, Nilli 1994). Several points sit at the wrong year: Raigorodskii's 561 is drawn at 1995 (it is 1997), Hinrichs's 323 at 2003 (2002), Hinrichs–Richter's 298 at about 2004 (2003), and Bondarenko's 65 at about 2012 (2013). Weißbach's 560 (2000) and Pikhurko's 321 (2002) are missing, and several heights are only rough (65 is drawn near 95, 298 near 250). The caption's "failed for dimensions above 2014" omits the 1325 that Kahn and Kalai also state.
- **Slide 7:** *fixed.* The original title "The Threshold is Dimension 9" and "finally located the floor" claimed the lowest failing dimension had been found. The revised slide is titled "Borsuk fails in dimension 9", places $X$ in $\mathbb{R}^9$, and says the conjecture fails in every dimension $d \ge 9$ while $d = 4,\dots,8$ remain open. Small blemishes: the last line of the bottom paragraph is clipped at the slide edge, and "definitive" and "breakthrough revealed" are breathless wording for an unreviewed preprint.
- **Slide 8:** *fixed.* The original drew the matrix $u u^{\top}$ wrongly (its first column read $u_1^2, u_1^2, u_2^2, u_4^2$) and asked "How do you build a 9-dimensional shape?". The revised matrix is correct (entry $u_i u_j$ in row $i$, column $j$, with $u_1^2,\dots,u_4^2$ on the diagonal), and the question now reads "How do you build a 3-dimensional set inside 9-dimensional space?". Small blemishes: the question runs to the right edge of the slide, and the tiny matrix inside panel 4 is still illegible decoration.
- **Slide 9:** no errors found.
- **Slide 10:** mostly right. "Our 9D shape $X$" has the same issue as slide 8, and "Translating 9D Spheres into Odd Maps" is vague; the paper builds an odd map *between* two 9-spheres.
- **Slide 11:** mostly right. The labels live on the lines through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^4$ (the space $\mathbb{RP}^3$), not on a "surface", and the blob picture is decorative.
- **Slide 12:** a simplification. An odd map between spheres of the *same* dimension hits every point; the bound "at least 10 labels" comes first, from the fact that an odd map cannot lower dimension (Proposition 4.1). That every maximal label set has exactly 4 labels needs the separate equality-case argument of Theorem 4.3.
- **Slide 13:** no errors in the text, except that a six-label triangle system is a set of ten triples, not a "graph". The picture (six outer labels joined by many lines around a 4-label square) is decorative and does not show a triangle system.
- **Slide 14:** a typo ("sito sizes"), and "when researchers followed" should credit the paper. The block picture (blocks of 3, 2, 2 and three spare labels $R$) matches the paper's Figure 3.
- **Slide 15:** *partly fixed, with new garbling.* The original's Lean panel was fictional: a theorem `borsuk_nine` with the wrong bound `b(X) > 9`, ending in `by sorry` under a green check mark, with the overclaim "completely formalized and machine-verified … The proof is undeniable". (The real challenge file `BorsukNine.lean` also ends in `by sorry` on purpose, because it only fixes the statement.) The revised Lean panel shows the real Comparator statement `OAI.BorsukNine.main_theorem`, written loosely (commas instead of `∧`): `IsCompact projectorSet`, `projectorSet ⊆ traceOneSymmetric`, `Metric.diam projectorSet = Real.sqrt 2` and `¬ HasTenSmallCover`. Its text says that `BorsukNine.lean` fixes the statement, that the proof is in the separate module `OAI.Geometry.Borsuk.Main`, and that the paper is an unreviewed preprint; "has been formalized" is slightly stronger than "listed as formalized", since the catalogue's review field reads `unchecked`. But the revision also broke the other two panels. The "Uncharted Zone" text is now gibberish ("The uncharted zone casually connqued nut impurtantly rocked to the violped strategicalct …"), so the slide no longer says that dimensions 4 to 8 are open. The "Exact Pieces" panel repeats the Lean text with a typo ("fixes the veic the statement") instead of saying that $b(X) > 10$ and that the exact number is unknown, and its picture is decorative. Slides 7 and 14 and the explainer cover those two points.

**`beginner-explainer-report.md`**

- **Section 1:** "a classic result proven by Borsuk for dimensions $d = 1, 2, 3$" is wrong. Borsuk proved that the ball needs $d+1$ pieces in *every* dimension. The conjecture holding for $d \le 3$ is due to Borsuk ($d = 2$, 1933) and to Perkal (1947) and Eggleston (1955) for $d = 3$. The history table's first row ("proved $b(Y) \le d+1$ for $d \le 3$") repeats the same error.
- **Section 2:** "grows exponentially relative to the dimension" is too strong: Kahn and Kalai's bound is $1.2^{\sqrt d}$, exponential in $\sqrt d$. The "Method / Approach Key" entries for Nilli, Grey–Weißbach, Raigorodskii, Weißbach, Pikhurko and Jenrich–Brouwer ("distance graphs", "geometric and combinatorial boundary reductions", "optimized spherical codes and lattice packings", …) are not in the paper and are unverified. "Resolved a long-standing boundary in geometric topology" is vague.
- **Section 3, worked example:** the 9-coordinate vector of $P_v$ is wrong. Its fourth coordinate is $\sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1/\sqrt2$, not $1$. The displayed distance calculation then contradicts itself ($\sqrt{1/2 + 1} = \sqrt{1/2+1/2} = 1$). With the correct coordinate the distance is $\sqrt{1/2 + 1/2} = 1$, which matches the Frobenius computation above it.
- **Section 4:** the new points of Corollary 7.1 are not "orthogonal points": they lie in an orthogonal copy of $\mathbb{R}^s$, but their Gram matrix is $I_s + \tfrac14\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$, so they are not mutually orthogonal.
- **Section 8:** "To guarantee absolute mathematical correctness … machine-checked" overclaims. The main theorem is listed as formalized and checkable with the Comparator tool, but the catalogue-wide review field reads `unchecked`, and this explainer did not re-run the build. `BorsukNine.lean` holds the Comparator challenge statement; the proof itself is in `OAI/Geometry/Borsuk/`.
- The proof walk-through in Section 5, including the partner-block contradiction, matches the paper.
