## Errata

NotebookLM is good at structure and visuals but sometimes invents or garbles mathematical detail. Known issues, found by reading each output against the papers:

**`beginner-explainer-report.md`**

This is the second report. It was generated from a prompt written as plain facts and replaced a first report that described the companion paper instead of the main one. It states Theorem 1.1, Corollary 7.1 and the parallel-search reduction correctly, and gives the companion its proper role as an input. Remaining issues:
- Section 1 says Hilbert asked for a "computer algorithm". He asked for a finite procedure; computers came later. Its reason why $\mathbb{Q}$ is hard ("fractions can possess arbitrarily large numerators and denominators") is not the real obstacle, since integers are unbounded too. The obstacle is the missing existential definition of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}$.
- Section 1's table lists rational solvability as "Proven **Undecidable** (OpenAI Paper, Sept 2026)". That is the preprint's claim. It is unformalized and not yet independently verified.
- Section 2 says Hilbert's tenth problem "specifically requires an existential definition". It doesn't. An existential definition was one sufficient route, and the paper stresses that undecidability does not require one.
- Section 2 dates Mazur's conjecture to 1992. That is correct even though it is not in the sources: Mazur posed it in "The topology of rational points", *Experiment. Math.* 1 (1992), and the paper cites his 1995 update. The same section says Poonen's 2009 definition uses "engineered families of elliptic curves". That is **wrong**: Poonen writes that "our proof uses not elliptic curves, but quaternion algebras". The section also calls Mazur's conjecture "widely believed to be true", which is **unverified**.
- Section 4's ASCII diagram labels the input "Polynomial f(x) with Integer Zeros". The input is any integer polynomial.
- Section 5, step 1 calls the finite tests "approximate rational solutions" and says $R$ contains "an infinite element $a$". The tests are exact, and the proof shows the root is in fact made of ordinary integers. The analogy with imaginary numbers is misleading.
- Section 6 calls the height of a ring element its "canonical height". The paper uses the Weil height $h$ for fractions and the canonical height $\hat h$ only for points on the curve. It calls $B$ "a complexity bound" (it is the largest index $\lvert\eta(a_j)\rvert$) and skips the key step, that $\delta = f(\eta(a))$ is divisible by $M(s)^K$.
- Section 6 says $\Phi$ is used "to maintain global consistency across all quadratic twists" and that Lemma 3.4 checks that "every scalar multiplier satisfies $\Phi$". Both are wrong. $\Phi$ forces even pole orders (Proposition 2.8), so that each contact prime matches a tested branch (Lemma 2.10). Lemma 3.4 produces rational points that show every *integer* satisfies $\Phi$.
- Section 7 lists "Selmer Corank Control" among the companion's results. The condition $s_2(E) \in \lbrace 0, 1\rbrace$ is a hypothesis of the companion's Theorem 1.1, not something it establishes.
- Section 8 says the paper "restricts the polynomial degree to $\deg(f) \le 4$". Degree 4 is an extra result (the problem stays undecidable for degree at most 4), not a restriction of Theorem 1.1.
- Section 9 says the paper is "undergoing rigorous peer review by the global number theory and logic communities". That is not in the sources and is **unverified**.
- It omits the proof of the five-point height estimate (the Shimura curve of discriminant 210 and modularity) and the second, conjugate-curve local comparison.

**`slides.pdf` / `slides.pptx`**
- The shipped deck is the revised one. The first deck stated the theorem wrongly on slide 6. On slide 8 it called compactness "assuming the impossible" and drew the elliptic curve with a self-crossing. Slide 10 said the height bound "only works for primes of ODD contact", slide 11 credited the pole-parity formula to the 2-converse, and slide 15 called ℚ a "continuum". A single revision fixed the text on all five and the curve on slide 8; the remaining problems are listed below.
- Slide 1: the subtitle "How Artificial Intelligence Settled Hilbert's Tenth Problem" overstates the status. The result is an unformalized preprint. The decorative formula is unrelated to the paper and slightly off. $(3x^3-7x^2+2x)/(x-2) = 3x^2-x$ holds only for $x \neq 2$, the minus sign is garbled, and there is a stray parenthesis. The listed values 0, 2, 10 are $3x^2-x$ at $x = 0, 1, 2$, and the last one is the value where the left side is undefined.
- Slide 2: $2x = 1$ is drawn as a slanted line. In the plane it would be the vertical line $x = 1/2$. The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 3$ (radius about 1.73) is drawn through grid points, which suggests integer points on it; there are none.
- Slide 3: "an inability to guarantee a stop is known as the Halting Problem" is loose. The halting problem is the question of whether a given program stops.
- Slide 4: "The first half of Hilbert's dream is dead" implies Hilbert asked about rationals too. His problem was about integers only. "The Integers (Z) are fundamentally undecidable" is loose: what is undecidable is integer solvability of polynomial equations.
- Slide 5: ℚ is drawn as an unbroken line and described as having "smooth, dense geometry". ℚ is dense but countable and full of gaps.
- Slide 6 (revised): now correct. It states Theorem 1.1, Corollary 7.1 (Turing degree 0′) and part (i) (degree at most 4). The only blemish is an extra "Gemini Notebook" logo in the corner.
- Slide 7: the labels "Program 1" and "Program 2" are each printed twice. The text box is correct, apart from the loose phrase "proved integers are unsolvable" (it is integer solvability of polynomial equations that is undecidable).
- Slide 8 (revised): the text is now correct: compactness, $T_a = \eta(a)P$, and "$\eta$ need not respect multiplication". The curve is now drawn smooth, as an oval plus an unbounded branch. Still wrong: the header prints "√2" as "sqrt(2̄)", and the label on the curve still says $n(a)$ instead of $\eta(a)$. The background formulas are decoration.
- Slide 9: says the truncated formal logarithm is used "on both the curve and its twin". In Lemma 2.3 the logarithm test is on $E$, and the conjugate-curve test compares formal parameters $z$. The small primes 2 and 3 shown as contact primes are among the excluded primes. Typo: "prime prime by prime".
- Slide 10 (revised): the text now states Theorem 2.4 correctly. $M(s)$ multiplies the odd-contact primes, and $h(s) \le H\cdot M(s)^c$ holds for every $s$ outside the five points. The picture was not redrawn. It still says "5 Branch Points" but draws eight endpoints (six of them ringed), with six label arrows. The background formulas are meaningless decoration.
- Slide 11 (revised): now correct. The parity restriction is elementary (Lemma 3.1), the 2-converse is used only to show that every integer satisfies $\Phi$ (Lemma 3.4), and $\Phi$ matches contact primes with tested branches (Lemma 2.10).
- Slide 12: the formulas are printed in plain-text style ("2*hhat(P)*B^2"), but the content is right. The background formulas are decoration.
- Slide 13: "We don't know the maximum number of variables needed" is a garbled version of the paper's point that its construction gives no bound on the number of variables. "No 'Many-One' Reduction" is too broad. The paper gives no many-one reduction from integer solvability to $\mathrm{H10}(\mathbb{Q})$, so it does not establish many-one completeness, but its degree-4 normal forms are many-one reductions from $\mathrm{H10}(\mathbb{Q})$.
- Slide 14: "A new era where machines solve the problems that human minds have hunted for a century" is promotional and not from the paper.
- Slide 15 (revised): the takeaway is now correct. Rational and integer solvability are both undecidable, with the same Turing degree, and the label reads "Undecidable over ℚ". Typo: "Turing degree 0prime" should read "Turing degree 0′". The background formulas are decoration.
- Slides 10–15 share background "chalkboard" formulas that mean nothing.
